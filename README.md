# Product-Space-Interview-Growth-Strategy

Project Description-
1. Executive summary

Thesis: Content is free everywhere (YouTube, podcasts, blogs). What’s scarce is access to practitioners and a sense of progress. That’s why an interview is a Watch → Practise → Track unit.
Three bets: Each fixes a diagnosed cause (practice loop for D1, role-based discovery for D4, credibility system for D3). I chose them because they’re low-effort, testable within weeks, and strengthen each other.
North star (WRIL): “Weekly Returning Interview Learners”. The brief’s goal is recurring use, so the metric must count return behaviour. I avoided raw views because autoplay and clips can inflate them. The 60% watch threshold is a starting hypothesis, and I said I’d calibrate it against real completion curves.
Weakness to own: WRIL excludes brand-new users, which is why my cost metric uses “new and returning”.
Assumptions box: I couldn’t see the product, so I said so. If the feature turned out to be something else, such as AI mock interviews, the strategy logic still holds and only the content engine changes.

2. Diagnosis (D1 to D4)

These are hypotheses about why interview content doesn’t bring people back: passive consumption, no rhythm, a trust gap, no personal path.
If asked how you’d test them: D1, look at what users do right after watching. D2, look at the time between interview visits. D3, ask trust questions in a survey. D4, look at the shelf’s click-through. That is what the first 30 days do.

3. Target users

A and B are primary because they have a deadline, actively want insider views, and have urgent need. Urgency drives frequency, and frequency is what makes a feature recurring.
C is secondary: good for brand and long-term retention, but a weaker acquisition engine.
Job to be done is a framework term. It describes the outcome the user wants rather than their demographics.
Honest answer if challenged: the segment choice is a hypothesis. In week 1 I’d check Product Space’s actual user mix.

4. Value proposition

Three pillars: insider signal (what was actually asked), role-model proof, and applied practice. Each one is something a blog or video can’t easily copy.

5. Distribution

P0/P1 are priority levels. P0 is do first, P1 is next.
Why in-product first: it’s cheapest and reaches people already interested.
Why guest co-distribution is also P0: guests share with their own networks, which brings borrowed credibility and reach.
Why SEO is later: it takes time to compound and its confidence is low.

6. Engagement loops

Hook model (Nir Eyal): trigger → action → variable reward → investment. L3 (users submit questions) is the “investment” part.
The diagram shows one learner journey: discover → watch → practise → compare → track → ask, then back to discovery. The final step feeds the next guest’s questions, which is what makes it a loop.
Why version 1 uses self-scoring: it needs no heavy engineering and tells us what learners find useful. AI feedback comes in version 2. Starting with AI feedback would be costly and unproven.
Why the streak is “light”: heavy gamification is in my “not now” list because it can distract from learning.

7. Content and credibility

Four pillars: verified, specific, independent, accountable. Trust is the real moat because one generic or promotional interview damages every other interview.
Quality scorecard (8 out of 10): the threshold is a starting standard I’d tune after seeing real ratings.
Content-loop diagram: one recording session becomes many assets (the interview, clips, an SEO page, practice questions), so cost per learning asset falls. Measuring drop-offs and ratings then tells us who to invite next.
“Atomise” just means breaking one piece of content into many smaller ones.

8. Metrics

Stages follow a funnel: discovery → activation → retention → credibility → loop health → business.
Guardrails are metrics that must not get worse while growth metrics improve.
Causality note: interview users may retain better simply because they were more motivated. A holdout group (a control that doesn’t get the feature) separates cause from correlation.
Targets are hypotheses. I said so in the document, and you should say so out loud.

9. Prioritisation and experiments

RICE = Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort. I scored qualitatively (High/Med/Low) because I had no data. Then I added a second test: does it strengthen the core loop?
“Not now” items (heavy gamification, AI-generated interviews) show I made trade-offs instead of listing everything.
Each experiment has a hypothesis, a design, and a ship-or-kill rule. E1’s 20% minimum and 25% goal are thresholds I’d confirm once I have a baseline. A/B test = random split between those who see the change and those who don’t.

10. Plan, economics and risks

30/60/90: learn first, build the habit second, scale third.
Economics formula: the feature is worth running if content cost is less than extra paid conversions × ARPU (average revenue per paid user) plus retention value. I didn’t invent inputs, because I don’t have them.
Risks: each has a mitigation. The cannibalisation risk is real: free interviews might reduce paid-course demand, so I track interview-to-paid conversion.
